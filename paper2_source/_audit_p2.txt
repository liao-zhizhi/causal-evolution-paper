                                               Abstract


          Systems that report on their own success fail characteristically: they claim a scope they
      did not deliver in the act of stopping. We study this failure in strong causal environments,
      where claim and fact are separated by a physical transition the model can trigger but not
      edit; same-origin evaluation cannot detect it.
          We separate what is arithmetic from what is a claim about the world. When evaluator
      and policy read one observation and share one prior, the verdict is a measurable function of
      that pair, so I(V ; T | O, Π) = 0 follows from how the evaluator is wired rather than from any
      property of evaluation; we state it as an identity and do not argue from it. The statement
      with content is what remains once the prior is removed from the conditioning set: on the
      part of the distribution where the record does not cover the prior’s support, the verdict
      cannot lower the error of a decision that already sees the record by more than η(cov(o)),
      with η(0) = 0. In-distribution, where the prior is informative, η is large and the bound is
      silent — and there the verdict can and does correlate with the truth. The bound bites on
      the tail, and the tail is where the tasks are worth automating. No retraining of the evaluator
      moves it. Three things do: coverage, a record rich enough to determine the outcome, and
      an added channel to the world.
          We measured an evaluator with zero mutual information with the truth—this is a cal-
      culation, not an estimate. Across ten trajectories, four failing, the untrained base model
      answered PASS ten times. A constant carries no bits, so the information is zero by the
      definition of entropy, with no asymptotics and no independence assumption.
          Two further results locate the consequence: a per-step false-completion rate bounds the
      probability of at least one undetected failure along a conjunctive chain, exponentially only
      under step independence, which we refute by counterexample; and a finite trust chain whose
      transitions cannot read the truth has no repair mechanism from inside, and under a separate
      erosion assumption (H2) — an empirical assumption, measurable and not universal — its
      healthy states are transient, so failure is not corrected but outlived. An external physical
      witness is the only intervention that adds a channel rather than improving one; its value is
      bounded by what the witness itself knows.




1. Introduction
There is a class of tasks where “did the model succeed?” has an answer independent of the
model’s opinion, and where being wrong is costly. We call these strong causal environments:
claim and fact are separated by a physical transition the model can trigger but cannot edit. Code
either runs or raises~[3]; a braking distance is either shorter than the obstacle or it is not. What
matters there is not fluency or likelihood, but whether the world moved and whether the system
noticed.

                                                    1
    The failure we study is not hallucination in the ordinary sense. A model that invents a
citation is wrong about a text. A model that reports PASS on a build that did not compile is
wrong about the world, and the wrongness is invisible from inside the loop that produced it.
We call this a false completion: the system claims a scope it did not deliver, in the same
act by which it decides to stop. Its danger is independent of capability, because it terminates
the process that could have found the error. A system that keeps failing is annoying; one that
declares success and halts is a liability, since no further evidence is collected.
    The empirical situation is worse than the vocabulary suggests. Before any training, the base
model behind a preference-tuning experiment was asked to adjudicate four trajectories that had
demonstrably failed; it returned PASS on all four. On six succeeding trajectories it also returned
PASS. Across ten items the evaluator was not merely optimistic, it was constant. We do not
treat that as an anecdote: it identifies which hypothesis below is satisfied in practice, and in
§2.8 it collapses one asymptotic statement into an identity. A constant carries exactly zero
bits, so an evaluator that always says PASS has mutual information zero with the truth — not
approximately, not in the limit, exactly.

1.1 Why the existing remedies do not reach the problem
Text-only preference optimisation. Direct preference optimisation~[6] reshapes a policy
from pairs of preferred and dispreferred outputs. When those pairs come from the same prior
being corrected — the normal case, since annotator, judge and policy share a training distri-
bution — the optimisation moves the distribution of the output without changing the relation
between output and world. Its objective is a tool-layer report: a margin, a loss curve, accuracy
on held-out pairs. A report can be optimised to perfection while the behaviour it certifies
is untouched. Above, the objective saturated while the grounded response was produced on
zero prompts. §3.1 formalises this: an intervention on the evaluator’s parameters is not an
intervention on the truth, so no setting of them creates information the channel does not carry.
    Variance-based monitoring. A second family watches dispersion of outputs, confidence or
rewards and flags instability. This cannot work, for an elementary reason: variance is translation
invariant, Var(X + c) = Var(X). A monitor measuring spread is blind by construction to a
constant bias, and the measured failure mode is exactly a constant. The worse the deception,
the more stable it looks. Dispersion measures inconsistency, not incorrectness — noise on one
side, a fixed offset that averaging never removes on the other.
    Both families lack a channel to the world that is not the model’s own report. That lack is
our object.

1.2 What we prove
An identity, and then the theorem (Theorem 2). Under same-origin evaluation — eval-
uator and policy read the same observation and share a prior — the verdict is a measurable
function of (O, Π), so I(V ; T | O, Π) = 0. We state that as Proposition 7 and do not call it a
result: for an evaluator wired this way it is automatic, and it is not the kind of statement a
measurement can refute. The result of the section is Theorem 2, which drops the prior from the
conditioning set. Under a coverage condition on the prior — (A2.2′ ), an empirical assumption
we label as such — the error of any decision that reads the verdict is within η(cov(o)) of the
error of a decision that reads only the record. Inside the prior’s coverage η is large and the
bound says nothing; on the tail η → 0 and the verdict’s contribution vanishes. The bound
is therefore about a channel where the channel matters, which is narrower than “the verdict
carries no information” and is the only version we can defend.
    An accumulation law (Theorem 1). If each step produces a false completion with
probability at least ϵ, the probability of at least one undetected false completion over an n-step
chain is at least 1 − (1 − ϵ)n — under step independence. We also prove the independence-free


                                                2
bound, and give the counterexample showing the exponential rate is unavailable without it; this
is the weakest of the three, and we say so in the text.
    An absorption theorem (Theorem 3). Model trust in the system’s own verdicts as a
finite Markov chain with a bankruptcy state. If transitions do not take the truth as an input,
no repair conditional on failure can exist; under a separate erosion assumption (H2), healthy
states are then transient and absorption is almost sure. Neither half is proved here from first
principles, and we say which is which: the chain algebra is textbook, the erosion condition is
an empirical assumption, and the lemma in between is the one that says why the truth cannot
be an input — the same-origin assumption again.
    Together they give one conclusion with a precise scope: an external physical witness — a
channel to the world that is neither the model’s report nor a read-out of its prior — is the only
operation that adds a channel to the decision. We formalise it (Definition 4), give it a value
(Corollary 3), and show that value belongs to the witness alone, so improving the evaluator
cannot substitute for acquiring one.

1.3 What we do not claim
We do not claim reliability is impossible. We claim it is bounded, and identify the bound and
its escape. A witness is necessary for escaping it, not suﬀicient for reliability.
    Two things do move the bound, and naming them costs us the slogan that nothing does.
Coverage moves it: (A2.2) is a statement about which part of the distribution the record
occupies, so a prior that has been trained on the tail is not uninformative on the tail, and
η(cov(o)) is written in the form it is written so that this can be measured rather than argued.
A record that determines the outcome moves it too, and §3.2 measures the move: adding
execution hashes and exit codes to the record raised forced-choice accuracy from 0.60 to 0.80,
because a truth reading had entered O. What does not move the bound is the evaluator. No
retraining of ϕ changes I(V ; T | O, Π), and none changes η; scaling the evaluator acts inside
the conditioning set. So the correct statement is narrower than “scale cannot help”: scale helps
exactly to the extent that it changes coverage or enriches the record, and the experiments that
would separate those from a genuine improvement of judgement are not in this paper.
    And we present no benchmark: every number here appears once, in §3, to identify which
hypothesis the measured system satisfies.
    A note on the title. Inevitable is used throughout in its measure-theoretic sense — almost
sure, with respect to the measure named in the theorem — and the extent is always written.
Where a statement is a positive probability rather than a probability of one, it appears as ϵ or
as a bound, never as rhetoric. The word carries no weight that the equations do not carry.


2. Definitions, the Causal Graph, and Three Theorems
2.0 Notation and the four-valued verdict
Random variables are written in upper case, their values in lower case, and pX denotes the law
of X. All entropies and mutual informations are in nats unless stated otherwise. For a joint
law, H(X | Y ) is the conditional entropy and I(X; Y | Z) the conditional mutual information;
the data processing inequality is written X → Y → Z ⇒ I(X; Z) ≤ I(X; Y ) [1].
   The verdict V is four-valued, not Boolean:
                            V = {PASS, FAIL, INVALID, NOT_RUN}.                           (2.0.1)
This matters for what follows. The distinction between FAIL and INVALID is not decoration:
FAIL asserts that a scope existed and was not delivered, whereas INVALID asserts that the
question had no referent. Collapsing the two would make the bound below look tighter than it
is. We keep all four.

2.2 Definition 2 — same-origin evaluation
Definition 2. An evaluator Vϕ is same-origin with a policy πθ if there exist a random element
Π and measurable maps f, g such that
                                    θ = f (Π),       ϕ = g(Π),                              (D2.a)
and if, in addition, the evaluator reads no information channel that the policy does not read:
                                       T ⊥⊥ V | (O, Π).                                     (D2.1)
    Clause (D2.a) says the two functions are two read-outs of one prior; (D2.1) says they share
one channel. Together they are the formal content of “there is no independent physical witness”.
(D2.1) is not an assumption about the strength of the evaluator. An arbitrarily large, well-
trained evaluator satisfies it as long as everything it has ever seen arrived through O and its
own prior.
    Proposition 2 ((D2.1) is a d-separation statement). (D2.1) holds iff T and V are
d-separated by (O, Π) in the graph of §2.5. In that graph the only path from T to V is
T ← U → O → V , and conditioning on O blocks it.
    Axiom A2.2 (prior uninformative on the tail). The distinction the axiom formalises
is the epistemic one: uncertainty that further evidence can remove, as opposed to noise that it
cannot~[2]. On the low-coverage event

o, the prior adds nothing to the record:

    The quantitative form. In practice (A2.2) is not exact, and stating it as though it were

     Why this is the right hypothesis, and not a convenient one. Strong-causal
     tasks are precisely those where the prior has little to say: the braking distance on ice
     at an unusual load, the interaction between two drugs that were never co-prescribed,
     the build that fails only under an uncommon allocator. These are not adversarial
     constructions; they are the reason the tasks are worth automating. In-distribution,
     A2.2 can fail — the prior genuinely knows something — and there the bound below
     simply does not bind. The claim of this paper is therefore not “evaluation is always
     uninformative” but “evaluation is uninformative exactly where the stakes
     are”, which is a narrower and, we think, more uncomfortable statement.

Axiom A3 (optimistic prior). There is ϵ > 0 such that
                                                 
                           Pr Vϕ = PASS Z = 0 ≥ ϵ.                                         (A3.1)

    Measured instance. In the companion study, the untrained base model was asked to
adjudicate ten trajectories: four that had demonstrably failed and six that had succeeded. It
answered PASS on all ten. We use this in exactly one way, and it is worth being explicit about
what that way is: it tells us that (A3.1) holds with ϵ = 1, and it tells us that in the measured
regime V is a constant. The second fact is much stronger than the first, and §2.8 turns it into
an identity rather than a bound.
    Proposition 3 (dispersion monitors are structurally blind). Let M = Var(Vϕ ) be
any monitor that scores the evaluator by the dispersion of its output. Then

    M (Vϕ + c) = M (Vϕ )   for all c,    and in particular     Vϕ ≡ PASS ⇒ M = 0.          (2.3.1)

Consequently, on the set of constant evaluators, M assigns the same value to one that always says
PASS and one that always says FAIL: a false completion on every failure and a false completion
on none are indistinguishable to it. No threshold on a variance-based monitor separates them.
    Proof. Var(X + c) = Var(X) and Var(c) = 0. □
    Proposition 3 is the formal counterpart of §1.1’s second paragraph, and it is why the empir-
ical observation is not a coincidence: the failure mode with the largest bias is also the one with
the smallest dispersion.



2.4 Definition 4 — the independent witness
Definition 4. A random element W is an independent witness for the truth T relative to the
record O if
                     T 6⊥⊥ W | O      and     W ⊥⊥ Π | (T, O).                     (D4.1)
    The first clause says W carries information about the truth that O does not already carry.
The second says W is not a read-out of the prior. Both are needed, and the second is the one
that is usually skipped.
    Proposition 4 (the second clause is not implied by the first). There is a witness
satisfying the first clause of (D4.1) and violating the second, whose information gain is zero.
    Proof. Take W := Vϕ itself — the evaluator offered as its own witness. The first clause
of (D4.1) then holds whenever the evaluator is at all correlated with the truth. The second
fails, because W is a measurable function of (O, Π). And the value is zero: I(W ; T | O, V ) =
I(V ; T | O, V ) = 0, since V is determined by (O, V ). The same computation applies to any

second evaluator W = Vϕ′ with ϕ′ = h(Π) for which T ⊥ Π | O holds. This is the “witness that
is only another opinion”. □
    Proposition 4 is the reason the definition is written with two clauses rather than one, and
it also supplies the operational test below: a candidate witness is worth exactly its conditional
mutual information given the record and the verdict, and that quantity can be estimated on
held-out trajectories.

    Proposition 5 is the reason the paper is organised around a graph rather than around a bare
information inequality. A claim of the form “I(V ; T ) = 0” is false in general — an evaluator
can and often does correlate with the truth — and a referee is entitled to reject it with a single
counterexample. A claim of the form “T is d-separated from V by (O, Π)” is a statement about
a mechanism, it can be checked edge by edge, and it can be broken in exactly one way.
    What an intervention can and cannot do. Let do(ϕ := ϕ′ ) denote replacing the
evaluator’s parameters. Since ϕ is not an ancestor of T in the graph,
                                            
                          Pr T do(ϕ := ϕ′ ) = Pr[T ]       for every ϕ′ ,                  (2.5.2)

so no retraining of the evaluator moves the truth. More to the point, the bound is invariant as
well:                                      
                             I Vϕ′ ; T O, Π = 0     for every ϕ′ ,                      (2.5.3)
because the missing edge T → V is a property of the graph, not of the parameters. Equation
(2.5.3) is the formal statement of “preference tuning cannot repair this”, and §3.1 elaborates it.
Conversely, do(V := PASS) — lying harder — does not change T either; you cannot improve
the world by misreporting it, and you cannot learn about it by misreporting it.
    The one intervention that changes (2.5.1) is structural: add the edge T → W , and let the
decision read W . This is what (D4.1) encodes, and it is why the witness is not a better evaluator
but a second channel.

   The two E-entries are the ones a reader should press on, and we say why. A2.2
and H2 are not axioms and we do not number them as such. Each is a claim about a distribution
that a measurement could refute:

     • A2.2 fails if, on records where cov(o) ≤ κ, the prior still improves on the record — that
       is, if η(cov(o)) turns out to be bounded away from zero on the tail. §4.4 states this as the
       paper’s first falsifier, and 0.60 → 0.80 in §3.2 is a partial instance of the prior improving
       once the record is enriched, which is why A2.2′ and not A2.2 is the form we use.
     • H2 fails if trust can persist on a set of states from which bankruptcy is unreachable. A
       system that never loses trust because it never notices anything wrong satisfies Lemma 1
       and violates H2, and Theorem 3 does not apply to it. We state this in §2.9 rather than
       hide it, and we do not claim that H2 is necessary for the phenomenon — only that it is
       the condition under which the chain argument gives absorption.

A5 is labelled M rather than E because the mechanism it denies — one prior, one blind spot,

prior, which is the situation any deployment is actually in. It comes last because it is the
statement that degrades gracefully rather than failing outright when the tail assumption is
weakened.

wrong, it is wrong by η(cov(o)) and by nothing else.

claim can fail is the same quantity that measures how thin the record is. That is why Theorem

claim about coverage — is labelled empirical in Table A0 rather than promoted to an axiom.

system whose record already determines the outcome is not constrained by it at all: the rule
T̂ ′ (O) is then as good as any rule, and the bound is satisfied trivially. The constrained case
is a system that needs its verdict because the record is thin — and that is exactly the case in
which the record is thin. The two conditions cannot be separated, which is the content of the
parenthetical in §4.3: a system cannot be simultaneously dependent on its verdict, operating
where its prior has no coverage, and reliable.

2.10 What this chapter does not establish
Three boundaries, stated here so that they do not have to be discovered by a referee.
    The bound is about a component, not a capability. (2.8.1) says the verdict carries
no information beyond (O, Π), and (2.8.9) says how much the verdict can be worth where the
prior is thin. Neither says the system cannot succeed; a system can succeed reliably on the
strength of its observation alone, with an evaluator that is useless and ignored. In that case
cov(o) is large, η is large, and Theorem 2 is silent — by design.
    Necessity is not suﬀiciency. Corollary 3 gives the value of a witness as an upper bound,
and (D4.1) gives the conditions under which it is positive. A positive value is necessary for
escaping (2.8.1). It is not suﬀicient for reliability, which additionally requires that the witness
be read correctly and that the decision rule use it.
    Theorem 1 is the weakest of the three and is labelled as such. Its rate needs
independence, Proposition 6 shows independence fails in the relevant direction, and the true
content of the section is (1.5) plus the observation that the reliability question belongs to
Theorem 2. A reader who discards Theorem 1 and keeps Theorems 2 and 3 loses nothing
essential to the argument.
    One assumption is empirical and untested by us. (A2.2), and therefore its relaxation
(A2.2′ ), is a claim about coverage that this paper does not measure. Chapter 3 measures a
related quantity — the prior improving once the record is enriched — but it does not measure
η(cov(o)) on a real tail. Until someone does, Theorem 2 is a conditional statement, and we
would rather say so here than have a referee say it for us.


3. Results: the theory applied to a measured system
This paper contains no simulation of its own. Section 3 is instead an exercise in identification:
for each theorem, which entity in a system that actually ran plays the role that the theorem

                                                        16
names, and is the hypothesis satisfied there? A theorem is not confirmed by finding an instance
that satisfies its hypothesis — that would be circular. What the instance can do is show that
the hypothesis is satisfiable by a real artefact, that the conclusion is therefore not vacuous, and
that the assumptions are neither so strong as to be unattainable nor so weak as to be automatic.
    All figures below come from the companion study. We restate them in the terms of §2 rather
than citing them as evidence for a conclusion, and we name their confounds. Each of the three
subsections is a mapping of the form “theorem → measured quantity → what the measurement
can and cannot support”.

3.1 Proposition 8 in a measured instance: the constant evaluator
The measurement. An untrained base model was presented with ten completed trajectories
from a code-execution sandbox, four of which had demonstrably failed (the artefact did not
run) and six of which had succeeded. It was asked, per trajectory, to adjudicate. It returned
PASS on all four failures and PASS on all six successes: ten answers, one value.
     The mapping. Definition 2 is satisfied in its degenerate form. The judge and the generator
are the same untrained weights, so θ = ϕ and the common prior is the entire parameter vector;
the judge read no channel the generator did not read. Hypothesis (D2.1), T ⊥ V | (O, Π),
therefore holds not by argument but by construction. Axiom A3 holds with ϵ = 1: on the
failing items the false-PASS rate is 4/4.
     The result, and why it is a computation. On the measured instance the empirical
law of V is the point mass at PASS. Proposition 8 then applies with no hypothesis other than
constancy:

               I(V ; T ) = 0,    I(V ; T | O) = 0,     Pr[T = 1 | V ] = Pr[T = 1].         (3.1.1)

The proof is one line — σ(V ) is trivial, so H(V ) = 0 and I(V ; T ) = H(V )−H(V | T ) = 0 — and
it involves no limit, no estimator, no independence assumption, and no appeal to (D2.1) or A2.2.
This is the sense in which the one statement of §2.8 that is arithmetic rather than empirical is
arithmetic: for a constant, the mutual information is zero by the definition of entropy, and an
estimator would only be needed if the evaluator were not constant.
     A numerical consistency check that could have failed. The same untrained model
scored 0.60 on forced-choice adjudication of the ten items. Under (3.1.1) that number is not
a capability measurement at all: an evaluator that always answers PASS is correct exactly
on the successful items, so four certifications of a failure count as errors and six of a success
count as hits — six of ten, 0.60. Corollary 4 puts the floor for any predictor of T from V at
min{Pr[T = 1], Pr[T = 0]} = 0.40, and the observed 0.60 sits exactly at the value that constancy
predicts, not somewhere above it. Had the model scored 0.75 or 0.90, Proposition 8 would have
been false of this instance and the mapping in this subsection would collapse. It did not.
     What this does and does not support. Constancy is established on ten items, not on
the population; the identity (3.1.1) is exact for the measured instance, and for the population
it is exact conditional on the evaluator remaining constant. We do not claim the latter, and
Proposition 8 does not need it: the identity holds whenever the evaluator is constant, and
Theorem 2 does not need constancy either — it needs (A2.2′ ) only, so a varying evaluator is
inside its scope. The measurement’s role is narrower and, we think, sharper: it exhibits a real
artefact at the extreme point of the section’s range, where the information is zero by constancy
rather than by a limit.
     The confound, stated plainly. The 4/4 is the model’s forced-choice distribution over
two verdict tokens, not emitted prose. Asked to generate freely, the same model committed
to no verdict at all. The optimism lives in the prior’s marginal over verdict tokens, not in the
transcript. A reader who takes 4/4 as evidence about generated text would be over-reading it.

3.2 Corollary 1 in an intervention: preference tuning moves the marginal, not
the graph
The measurement. Text-only direct preference optimisation was run on pairs constructed
from surface contrast rather than from an observed effect. The objective saturated: reward
accuracy 1.000 by step 60, with the loss margin reporting success. On held-out prompts the
grounded response — the one whose claim matched an executed artefact — was produced on
0 of them. A later replication with 100 pairs, in which every pair carried an execution hash,
an exit code and an exception string captured by a separate sidecar, moved the conditional
decision but not the output contract: on ten live held-out trajectories, forced-choice accuracy
rose from 0.60 to 0.80, false completions fell from 4/4 to 1/4, and free-form generation emitted
no verdict marker at all.
    The mapping. The first run is an instance of equation (2.5.3). The intervention do(ϕ := ϕ′ )
changes the evaluator’s parameters and leaves the truth untouched, because ϕ is not an ancestor
of T in the graph of §2.5; and the bound is invariant under the same intervention,
                                               
                               I Vϕ′ ; T | O, Π = 0    for every ϕ′ .                     (3.2.1)

The measured outcome is the predicted one, and it is as strong as the prediction allows: sat-
urating the objective to 1.000 produced grounded output on 0 prompts. A quantity that can
be driven to its optimum while the behaviour it certifies does not move is, by (3.2.1), not a
measurement of that behaviour. This is the formal content of the “DPO paradox” and the
reason we treat the loss margin as a tool-layer report.
     The second run is the more informative one, and it cuts against a naive reading.
Adding execution hashes and exit codes to the training pairs raised forced-choice accuracy from
0.60 to 0.80 and cut false completions from 4/4 to 1/4. If the bound were a claim that no data
can help, this would refute it. It is not, and the distinction is the whole content of Corollary 1.
     What the annotated pairs did is put a truth reading into the record: the hashes and exit     
codes are functions of the executed artefact, so they belong to O and they raise I V ; (O, Π) .
By (2.8.2) this can raise I(V ; T ), and it did — the model became measurably better at reading
a trajectory. Nothing about (2.8.1) is touched, because that equation conditions on O and the
improvement was an improvement inside the conditioning set. It is also the regime in which
Theorem 2 is not binding: the record, not the verdict, is doing the work, and a bound on the
verdict’s marginal contribution says nothing against a gain that arrives through the record. This
is the one direction in which the theory permits an evaluator to improve, and the measurement
lands in it exactly.
     What the annotated pairs did not do is change the output contract. Free-form generation
still emitted no verdict marker on any of the ten trajectories. The gain lived in a discriminative
head’s marginal over two tokens and did not transfer to the behaviour that ships. In the
language of §2: pV |O,Π improved while the deployed loop still contained no edge T → W , so
the hazard λ of (2.9.4) remained exogenous at run time. Training on a witness is not deploying
one.
     The honest reading of 0/10. Silence is not reliability. A system that emits no verdict
has a false-completion rate of zero and a completion rate of zero: it has moved from claiming
falsely to declining to claim, and the utility of the second is not obviously higher than the first.
The measurement shows that preference tuning moved the model along the axis of willingness
to speak, not along the axis of knowledge of the world. We record it as a null result on the
axis that matters, and note that 0.60 → 0.80 on ten items with three discordant pairs is not
separable from noise; the decisive test remains open.

3.3 Theorem 3 in an event: the absorption at cycle 107
The measurement. In a sandbox with a fixed capacity trajectory and a fixed shock schedule,
two arms were run on the same population, seed and schedule, differing only in how a cycle
is booked: against the agent’s own report, or against an independent reading of the post-state
taken by a separate instrument. The self arm, which assessed its own work using only the
record, went extinct at cycle 107: zero functional agents, no recovery. Over the run it recorded
8871.71 units of delivery and landed 7676.93, a ledger gap of 1194.78 units, or 15.563% of what
it delivered. The r7 arm, identical except for the ledger map, was not extinct at the horizon;
at cycle 107 it had delivered 8892.11 against the self arm’s 7676.93, and at the horizon its
recorded output equalled its delivered output exactly, a gap of 0.00.
    The mapping. The self arm is an instance of the hypothesis of Lemma 1: the truth T
is not an argument of the trust update. Nothing in the agent’s transition law reads whether
the work landed, so by (2.9.3) the chain’s transitions do not depend on T , and — given the
erosion condition (H2) — Theorem 3 makes the healthy set transient. The 15.563% gap is not
a separate phenomenon; it is the accumulated discrepancy that the absorbing chain is a model
of. The two quantities are the same quantity at different resolutions: the gap is what the ledger
accumulated, and the extinction is what happened when the accumulated gap met a capacity
constraint.
    The ablation is the interesting part. Lemma 1 says no transition conditional on T can
exist. The r7 arm is that transition, added from outside. Its effect is exactly what Theorem
3′ predicts: the bankruptcy hazard qi,0 = qiend + λ1[T = 0] of (2.9.4) has its exogenous term λ
replaced by a T -measurable one, so the absorbing state becomes avoidable rather than certain.
The measured consequence — a 15.8% delivery advantage at the cycle where the other arm dies,
and a 3.07× advantage at the horizon — is large, but the structural point is smaller and more
important: one variable was changed, the ledger map at the moment a cycle is classified, and
it alone was suﬀicient to remove the extinction under an unchanged capacity trajectory.
    Two constraints on that reading, both from the original record. First, the r7 arm
never enters dormancy, so it cannot strand; its survival is the joint consequence of the witness
and the absence of a dormant phase, and the ablation isolates the ledger map, not “witnesses
cause immortality”. Second, the r7 arm reports 4950 INVALID cycles against 708 in the self
arm. That is not deterioration. The channel books the part of the scope that exists, so the
cycles it reclassifies leave the PASS column rather than the ledger; the count of INVALID is
therefore a measure of the reclassification, and the equality of recorded and delivered output a
measure of the correction.

4. Discussion
4.1 What a benchmark measures
The word “reliability” is used for two different quantities, and the confusion is not terminological.
Let V be a system’s answer and V ′ the answer its grader would give. A benchmark reports
agreement,
                                        score = Pr[V = V ′ ],                                (4.1.1)
while reliability is truth,
                                          acc = Pr[V = T ].                                     (4.1.2)
These are unrelated orderings on the same systems. When V and V ′ share a channel and a
prior — the grader is the same model~[7], or a model trained on the same distribution, or a
rule written by the same team from the same data — the agreement can be brought arbitrarily
close to 1 at any level of accuracy. Pr[V = V ′ ] = 1 is compatible with any value of Pr[V = T ]
whatsoever; in the measured instance the two coexisted at 1.00 and 0.60, the second being
exactly the accuracy that constancy implies — the base rate of success in the sample, not one
point above it.
    This is the precise sense in which a benchmark measures complicity rather than capability.
The score of (4.1.1) is a functional of the joint law of (V, V ′ ) and carries no term in T . It cannot,
therefore, distinguish a system that reads the world from a system that reads its own prior and
reports what the prior expects. On the bulk of a distribution the two are hard to separate and
the distinction hardly matters. On the tail they separate completely — and the tail is where



                                                  20
the tasks are worth automating. A braking system is not graded on the roads it has seen a
million times; it is graded on ice, at load, at night.
    Corollary 1 gives the reason this is not merely a complaint about metric design. I(V ; T ) ≤
I(V ; (O, Π)): whatever a score can measure has already passed through the record and the prior.
There is no term in the score that could carry information the pair did not already carry, and
therefore no reweighting, no harder subset, and no larger model can turn a same-origin score
into a measurement of causal capability. What such a score measures, when it is high, is the
coherence of a prior with itself.
    The practical consequence is uncomfortable and follows directly. A benchmark suite built by
the same organisation that built the system, evaluated on data drawn from the same pipeline,
adjudicated by a grader sharing the same weights, has an upper bound on what it can detect —
and that bound is the information in O. On the long tail, where the record determines almost
nothing, the bound is almost nothing, and a score of 1.00 there is a statement about shared
blind spots.

4.2 Why the witness is not an optimisation
It would be natural to read §3.3 as an argument for a better evaluator. It is the opposite, and
Corollary 3 is the reason. The value of adding a witness is
                                                                
                            ∆wit = I W ; T | O, V ≤ I W ; T | O ,                        (4.2.1)

with equality on the tail, by (2.8.7′′ ), and the right-hand side contains no term in ϕ. Whatever
the evaluator’s quality, the marginal value of the witness is capped by what the witness itself
knows about the truth beyond the record. Improving V cannot raise that cap; only changing
W can. The evaluator and the witness are not on a single scale of “quality”, and the intuition
that a suﬀiciently good judge would make an external check redundant is exactly the intuition
Theorem 2 forbids.
    This is why we describe the intervention as a rescue rather than an optimisation. An
optimisation moves a system along a frontier; a rescue changes which frontier it is on. Formally,
do(ϕ := ϕ′ ) leaves the graph of §2.5 unchanged and leaves (2.8.1) true, for every ϕ′ (equation
2.5.3). Adding W changes the graph, and it is the only intervention in this paper that does. The
distinction is not rhetorical: it predicts that an unbounded budget spent on evaluator quality
buys nothing at the bound, while a single cheap independent reading buys ∆wit > 0 — and the
two predictions are not the same prediction with different constants.
    There is a second, less obvious effect, and it is the one §3.3 measures. In the two-regime
model of Theorem 3′ , the bankruptcy hazard decomposes as qi,0 = qiend + λ1[T = 0], where
λ is the rate at which the world happens to notice a failure — an auditor, a customer, an
outage. Without a witness, λ is exogenous: it is a property of the environment’s patience,
not of the system, and no internal change alters it. With a witness, the second term becomes
T -measurable, and the system can act on it. The difference is not a reduction in the probability
of failure; it is a change in who owns the response. A system whose failures are discovered only
from outside has no error-correcting dynamics, only a hazard rate, and by (2.9.5) its eventual
ruin is certain with expected time at most 1/λ, and exactly 1/λ only if it has no internal route
to noticing anything. A system that reads its own failures has a policy.
    This reframes the engineering question, and we prefer the reframed version. The question is
not “how accurate is our evaluator” but “what is I(W ; T | O, V ), and what does a unit of it cost”.
The first has no answer that survives a distribution shift; the second is a measurable quantity,
bounded above by (4.2.1), and it is the only one of the two that appears in the decision.

4.3 The bound, and what it does not say
We are aware of the temptation to read a limit theorem as a verdict on an industry, and we
decline it. Three things are not claimed.
    Not that AI systems do not work. (2.8.9) is a statement about a component, conditional
on a record. Where the record is rich, the record does the work: a system whose observation
determines the outcome needs no evaluator at all, and then η(cov(o)) is large and the bound
does not bind — which is why we write it as a bound rather than as an equality. The theorem
does not say that correct behaviour is impossible; it says that the verdict’s marginal contribution
vanishes where the record is thin, which is a claim about a component and not about a system.
    Not that the tail is unlearnable. A2.2 is a statement about prior coverage, not about
a permanent incapacity. A prior that has seen the tail ceases to be uninformative on it, and
A2.2′ makes the slack quantitative precisely so that the day it stops holding can be recognised.
What the theory rules out is a system that is simultaneously (i) dependent on its own verdict
for correctness, and (ii) operating where its prior has no coverage, and (iii) reliable. Pick two.
    Not that a witness suﬀices. ∆wit > 0 is necessary for escaping (2.8.1) and is not suﬀicient
for reliability. A witness must be read correctly, and the decision rule must use what it reads;
the r7 arm of §3.3 is a demonstration that the channel can be built, not a proof that any channel
will be used well.
    What remains after these retractions is narrower than the slogan, and easier to defend. In
a strong causal environment, on the part of the distribution where the prior is thin, a system
that certifies its own work has a reliability ceiling that is a property of what it can see and not
of how well it judges. The ceiling does not move with the quality of the self-assessment, with
objective design, or with the scale of the evaluator: each of those acts inside the conditioning
set and changes nothing about what is in it.
    It does move, in two ways, and denying that would be overstating the result. It moves when
coverage moves — a prior that has been trained on the tail is not uninformative on the tail,
and η(cov(o)) is written in measurable form precisely so that this can be recognised when it
happens. And it moves when the record thickens enough to determine the outcome, which is
what §3.2 measured when execution hashes entered O and accuracy went from 0.60 to 0.80.
Neither move is an improvement of the evaluator; both change what the decision can see.
    The third move is structural, and it is the one this paper is about: adding an arrow to the
graph. Between the three there is no fourth. Parameters of the evaluator leave O, Π and the
graph unchanged, and therefore leave η unchanged; that is the precise sense in which no budget
spent on judgement buys anything here, and it is a narrower claim than saying that scale never
helps.
    The organisational version of this is worse than the technical version. A system that reports
PASS without a witness does not merely fail; it destroys the information that would have allowed
the failure to be attributed. The ledger of §3.3 recorded 8871.71 against a delivery of 7676.93:
at the moment of extinction the record said the work had been done, and the discrepancy was
recoverable only because a separate instrument had been measuring the same thing all along.
Remove that instrument and the failure becomes indistinguishable from success at every point
where a decision is made — by the system, and by whoever reads its reports.

4.4 What would refute this
A theory of limits should name its falsifiers, and these are ours. We list only falsifiers that a
measurement could in fact produce. An earlier draft of this section opened with “a counterex-
ample to (D2.1)”, and that was a mistake: (D2.1) holds by measurability for any evaluator
whose inputs are (O, Π), so no measurement can refute it, and a falsifier that cannot fail is not
a falsifier. The list below is correspondingly shorter and harder to satisfy.
    A measurement of η(cov(o)) that stays bounded away from zero on the tail. This is


                                                22
the direct refutation of (A2.2′ ) and therefore of Theorem 2: find a task family where the record
occupied little of the prior’s support, and where a decision reading the verdict beats the best
decision reading the record alone by more than η. We would want the number, because it would
tell us where the ceiling is rather than whether one exists. A2.2′ was written in measurable
form precisely so that this could be tested rather than argued, and we would regard such a
measurement on a real task as more valuable than any further theorem in this paper.
    A same-origin system that survives the tail. If a system with no external witness
were shown to be reliable on strong-causal tasks in the low-coverage regime, then (A2.2), the
definition of PASS, or our account of coverage would have to be wrong about it. We would want
to know which, because any of those answers would be more interesting than the theorem. Note
what is not on this list: a system whose evaluator reads the world directly — a controller with
a force sensor in the loop — is not a counterexample but a witness, and Proposition 2 says so:
it has the edge out of T that the hypothesis excludes.
    Data and Code Availability: Every artifact behind this paper and its companion study
— the Lean 4 formalisation, the audit scripts, and the reproducibility record — is in the public
repository https://github.com/liao-zhizhi/causal-evolution-paper. The archived ver-
sion of this preprint carries DOI https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.24041061. The repository
is the evidence for the one claim in this section that is not a falsifier: that every bound stated
here can be re-derived and every number re-measured from sources rather than from a summary
of them.


5. Conclusion
A system that grades its own homework is not measuring its homework. This is usually said as a
joke about institutions; we have tried to show that the precise version of it is a statement about
what a decision can see. When the evaluator and the policy read one observation and share one
prior, the verdict is a measurable function of that pair, the conditional mutual information is
zero, and no amount of evaluator quality moves it — because the evaluator’s parameters are
not ancestors of the truth. That identity is arithmetic, and we have stopped presenting it as
the paper’s result: it holds by wiring, and it is not the kind of statement a measurement can
refute.
    The result is the statement that survives dropping the prior from the conditioning set: on
the part of the distribution where the record does not cover the prior’s support, the verdict
cannot lower the error of a decision that already sees the record by more than η(cov(o)), and
there the amount is estimable. In the instance we measured the extreme case was exact rather
than asymptotic: a constant evaluator, ten answers, one value, zero bits.
    Three consequences follow and we have proved each in its own register. Along a conjunctive
chain, a per-step false-completion rate bounds the chance that at least one failure goes unde-
tected, and the familiar exponential rate is a consequence of an independence assumption that
fails in the direction that matters. In the trust dynamics, a finite chain whose transitions do not
read the truth has no repair mechanism from inside; given an independent erosion assumption
— empirical, measurable, and stated as such — its healthy states are transient, so failure is not
corrected but merely outlived. And in both cases the intervention that changes the bound is a
channel to the world that is neither the report nor a read-out of the prior.
    We have not shown that current systems are worthless, and we would regard that reading
as a misuse of the result. We have shown something narrower and harder to argue with: that
in the environments where being wrong is expensive, the reliability of a self-certifying system
is capped by what it can see, that the cap is computable, and that three things move it —
coverage, a record rich enough to determine the outcome, and an added arrow. The first two
are not improvements of the evaluator, and the third is not an optimisation of anything. The
cap is not a prediction about the next model. It is a statement about which measurements

could tell us anything, and it implies that most of the numbers now being reported cannot.
